# meeting-prep

Most meetings fail because the participants did not decide in advance what counts as success. They show up open, the conversation drifts, the meeting ends, and everyone agrees to "follow up." Nothing happens.

A prepared meeting has a defined outcome before it starts. This skill produces that brief.

## The contract

Output is one page, exactly six sections, in this order:

1. **Their context** — 3–5 sentences about the other party, drawn from public sources or prior interactions. Refresh your memory; do not start cold.
2. **Your stated outcome** — one sentence. What does success look like at the end of the call?
3. **Their likely outcome** — one sentence. What do *they* want? If you can't guess, you haven't researched.
4. **Three questions** — ranked. Each must produce information you genuinely need. Each must be answerable in under two minutes.
5. **One ask** — the specific thing you will request. Yes or no. Not "let's continue the conversation."
6. **Walk-away condition** — under what circumstance do you end the call without further follow-up.

If you cannot fill in section 2 or 5, the meeting should not happen. Defer or cancel.

## Section 1 — Their context

Three to five sentences. Specific. Sources cited inline.

For a prospect:
- Company name, structure, scale.
- Recent moves (capital raise, acquisition, key hires).
- Operator background — who they are, where they came from, what they reward.
- Stated priorities in the last 60 days (LinkedIn posts, podcast appearances, press).

For an existing client:
- Last engagement outcome.
- Current state of their AKHARA deployment.
- Recent friction or wins.

For an investor or partner:
- Their portfolio thesis.
- Their last three deals.
- Their stated bar for the round/relationship.

If you do not know any of this and the meeting is in 24 hours, the meeting is premature. Do 30 minutes of homework or push it.

## Section 2 — Your stated outcome

One sentence. Specific enough to be objectively true or false at end of call.

Good outcomes:
- "Receive a signed proposal."
- "Schedule the next call with their CFO included."
- "Get explicit no-go on the partnership so I can stop spending cycles."
- "Confirm budget range so I know which tier to quote."

Bad outcomes (rewrite or refuse):
- "Have a good conversation." (Unmeasurable.)
- "Discuss the opportunity." (No outcome.)
- "Build rapport." (Not a meeting goal; a side effect.)
- "Learn more about their needs." (Discovery is fine but should be specified — "learn three things: budget, timeline, decision-maker.")

## Section 3 — Their likely outcome

One sentence. What do they want from this meeting?

Often it is:
- A specific question answered (price, scope, timeline).
- A reassurance about a perceived risk.
- An ego-validation (being talked to by someone they respect).
- A way out of further commitment.

If their outcome conflicts with yours (you want a yes, they want an exit), name that explicitly. The meeting strategy diverges in that case.

## Section 4 — Three questions

Three is the cap. More than three is wishful thinking.

Each question should:
- Open the topic that matters most for your outcome.
- Allow a substantive 60–120 second answer.
- Be unaskable elsewhere (i.e., not "what's your budget" if budget is on their pricing page).

Rank them. If the meeting goes long and you only get to one, it should be the highest-leverage.

Question quality test: would the answer change what you propose? If no, it is a discovery question, not a strategic question. Bump it down or drop it.

Examples (qualified buyer call):
1. "What's the single biggest operational gap you'd address if budget weren't the constraint?"
2. "Who else needs to sign off, and what do they need to see?"
3. "What does the next 90 days look like — is this a now decision or a Q4 decision?"

Examples (existing client check-in):
1. "What's working, in your words, and what would make you cancel?"
2. "What did your team try to do this month that the AKHARA setup got in the way of?"
3. "Where does this engagement need to be by Q4 for you to renew without thinking?"

## Section 5 — One ask

The single, specific request you will make before the call ends.

Form: "[Action] by [date]."

Examples:
- "Sign the proposal by Friday."
- "Send me the calendar invite for the CFO call by EOD Wednesday."
- "Confirm budget tier in writing by Tuesday."
- "Email me the intro to [name] by Monday."

If you have no specific ask, you have no reason to be on the call. Define the ask before, not during.

## Section 6 — Walk-away condition

Under what specific circumstances do you end the meeting without further follow-up?

Examples:
- "If they cannot name a specific timeline within Q3, walk."
- "If they ask for a free trial, walk."
- "If three people in the room have veto power and none of them is on the call, walk."

The walk-away is the most underused tool in meetings. Most operators stay engaged out of optimism. The walk-away condition is the pre-commitment to stop.

## Output format

```
MEETING PREP — [Their company / name]
[Date, time]

1. THEIR CONTEXT
[3-5 sentences, sourced]

2. YOUR OUTCOME
[1 sentence]

3. THEIR OUTCOME
[1 sentence]

4. THREE QUESTIONS (ranked)
A. [highest leverage]
B. [...]
C. [...]

5. THE ASK
[Action by date]

6. WALK-AWAY
[Condition]

ONE LINE TO REMEMBER:
[The single phrase you want to leave them with — usually an AKHARA-voice line]
```

## On the "one line"

A great meeting plants one phrase in the other party's memory. Pick it in advance.

For prospects: usually a frame ("AKHARA does not sell hours. It sells departments.")
For clients: usually a forward statement ("By month six, we are testing white-label.")
For partners: usually a specific bet ("We will hit $XX MRR in 12 months. Want in?")

The line should be sayable in under five seconds and be quotable.

## Refusals

Refuse to prep:
- A meeting with no stated outcome.
- A meeting on the same day (no time to research).
- A meeting with more than three attendees where the user is the only AKHARA representative — that is a panel, not a meeting; restructure.
- A meeting where the user has already had three with the same party with no outcome — at that point, the format is wrong; switch to a written proposal or end.

## The brutal version

Meetings are expensive. Real meetings cost the salaries of everyone in the room plus the opportunity cost of what they were not doing. A 30-minute meeting with 3 senior people is roughly $1,000 in time. Spend 15 minutes on prep and you 10x the ROI of the meeting itself.

Show up with the outcome decided. Run the meeting toward it. Leave with the ask answered.
