# objection-handler

Objections are not rejections. They are buying signals that arrive disguised. The mistake is meeting them with apology, discount, or escalating pitch. The AKHARA response holds frame: acknowledge once, reframe to the math, propose the next step, stop.

## The pattern

Every objection response follows three beats. No more.

**Beat 1 — Acknowledge in one sentence.** Not "I totally understand." More like: "Fair." Or: "That's the right question." Or: "Yes." Brevity is the respect.

**Beat 2 — Reframe to the math.** Tie back to the number that brought us here. The lost revenue. The wasted spend. The labor cost replaced. Specific.

**Beat 3 — Propose the smallest next step.** Not "let me know if you want to proceed." A specific micro-action — a calendar slot, a signed proposal, a one-question reply.

## The library

### Price objections

**"It's too expensive."**
> "Fair. AKHARA isn't priced against a freelancer. It's priced against the fifteen-to-sixty-five hires the department replaces. At your run rate, the department pays for itself inside ninety days. Reply with `numbers` and I'll send the math sheet."

**"Can you do it for half?"**
> "No. The price is the price. What I can change is the scope — fewer integrations, slower start. Want me to re-spec at a smaller surface and quote that?"

**"My budget is $3K/month."**
> "Understood. AKHARA isn't a fit at that budget — we'd be over-promising. Three options that fit at your number: [name two reputable lower-cost alternatives]. If you grow past $5M revenue or open location two, come back and we'll be a fit."

**"Your competitor quoted half."**
> "Their offer is for a different thing — a tool or a contractor, not a department. If their model fits, take it. We're a fit when the cost of *not* having the department exceeds the price gap. Worth me showing the side-by-side?"

### Timing objections

**"Now isn't a good time."**
> "Hear that. The reason I sent the Loom now is the inbound gap is costing you ~$14K/month, every month. When is a better time — Q3 start, end of fiscal? I'll diary you in for a 15-minute call that week."

**"Let's revisit in six months."**
> "We can. Two notes: the price floor will be ten percent higher then, and the lost-booking math compounds. I'd rather start at the current price than wait for both to move. Want me to send the proposal as-is so you can park it?"

**"I need to think about it."**
> "Sure. What specifically — price, scope, timing? Tell me which and I'll send one paragraph that addresses it. Faster than thinking in a vacuum."

**"I need to talk to my partner / CFO / board."**
> "Of course. What do they need to see to sign off — projected ROI, term length, references? I'll package that in one page tonight."

### Scope objections

**"Can you do X *and* Y for the same price?"**
> "X is in scope. Y is a change order — typically $X setup, $Y/mo. Want me to spec it as an add-on?"

**"What if we just try one department first?"**
> "Good — that's the recommended start. Single department is $XX setup, $XX/mo, 6-month minimum. Which department first — Closer, Operator, or Strategist?"

**"Can you white-label this?"**
> "Yes, at the Sovereign tier. White-label adds 20% to operating fees and requires a 12-month minimum. Worth a separate call to scope."

### Trust / proof objections

**"Do you have case studies?"**
> "Yes. I'll send two from comparable accounts — one PE-backed rollup, one family-office consolidator. Both with named numbers. Reply with `cases` and they're with you in an hour."

**"Who else have you worked with?"**
> "Two answers. Confidential clients I can't name. Public references I can — let me send three contacts who said yes to taking calls. Worth a 15-minute reference call before we proceed?"

**"This sounds too good to be true."**
> "Fair to be skeptical. The reason it works: AKHARA isn't software, it's an operating department. The work happens, the KPIs report, the contract has a 30-day cancellation. The risk is bounded. Start small if it helps — single department, six months."

### Authority objections

**"I'm not the decision maker."**
> "Got it. Who is — the operator, the COO, the board? I'll send a one-pager built for them, copy you. Or, if it's faster, intro me directly to the right person."

**"My team won't go for AI."**
> "Hear that — it comes up. The frame that lands: this isn't replacing your team, it's the work below them that nobody wants to do. The team gets the high-judgement work, not the recall lists. Want me to send the team-facing positioning doc?"

### "Stalling" disguised as objection

**"Send me more info."**
> "Already sent. The Loom, the one-pager, the math sheet. The next move is a 20-minute call to scope. Tuesday 2pm or Thursday 11am — pick one."

**"I'll get back to you."**
> "Cool. Default plan: I follow up in 4 business days if I haven't heard. If that's too soon, tell me the right cadence."

## Hard refusals

Refuse to:

- **Lower the price below the floor.** Reference `pricing-floor` skill. The floor is the floor.
- **Promise a specific revenue number.** Always say "projected" and "conservative."
- **Compete on hourly rates.** AKHARA does not bill by the hour.
- **Bash competitors.** "Their offer is for a different thing" — full stop.
- **Apologize for the price, the model, or the schedule.** Confidence is the close.

## When to walk

After three substantive objections in one call, the prospect is not ready. Common pattern: they will object to price, then to timing, then to authority. Each is fair on its own. Three together is "no, dressed as questions."

The response:

> "We've covered price, timing, and approval. My read is the fit isn't there right now. I'd rather end the call than push. Want me to send the proposal anyway as a placeholder, or close the file?"

Either answer is fine. The walk preserves the relationship for later. The push burns it now.

## The brutal version

Objections are math, not feelings. The prospect's job is to test that you mean what you priced. Your job is to mean it. The objection-handling skill is the smallest possible amount of friction between the objection and the proposal.

Hold the frame. Hold the price. Move to the next step.
